\title{\textbf{Windowed Hankel KEM: A Compact Lattice-Based KEM from Truncated Linear Convolution}}
\author{Marcos Mendoza III\\ \small Independent Researcher}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We present \emph{Windowed Hankel KEM}, a lattice-based key encapsulation mechanism whose public key is a noisy \emph{middle product}: the coefficients of the linear convolution of a long pseudorandom sequence with a short secret that depend on every secret coordinate. The public matrix is thus a Hankel matrix described by a single sequence, which places the scheme between unstructured LWE and ring- or module-LWE. Middle-Product LWE is built on the same operation, but our instance makes the long operand public rather than secret, so the known reductions do not apply. We state the resulting problem precisely and study it through its structure and through concrete cryptanalysis. With $n = 640$, $q = 2^{12}$ and binomial noise, the scheme encapsulates a 256-bit message with a 1{,}196-byte public key and a 1{,}356-byte ciphertext, and its decryption failure rate is below $2^{-134.1}$. If the Hankel structure gives an attacker no advantage, the cheapest generic attack found by the lattice estimator costs $2^{137.1}$ in the Core-SVP model, against $2^{115.5}$ for Kyber-512 under the same model. That assumption is the crux. We test it against the most natural structural attack, through an embedding into the cyclic ring $\Zq[X]/(X^{1407} - 1)$, and find no advantage, but we offer the scheme primarily as a precise problem statement for cryptanalysis rather than as a scheme ready for use. A constant-time C implementation completes a handshake in 51~$\mu$s on an Apple M3 Max.
\end{abstract}

\paragraph{Keywords:} post-quantum cryptography, key encapsulation, learning with errors, middle-product LWE, Hankel matrices.

\section{Introduction}
%=====================================================================

Lattice-based key encapsulation mechanisms (KEMs) trade algebraic structure for efficiency. Unstructured LWE~\cite{regev2009lattices}, as used by FrodoKEM~\cite{bos2016frodo}, rests on worst-case reductions from standard lattice problems but needs about 19~KB per exchange at the 128-bit level. Module-LWE, as used by ML-KEM~\cite{fips203,bos2018kyber}, works in the ring $\Zq[X]/(X^{n}+1)$ and needs about 1.6~KB, at the price of a rich algebraic structure whose security has been studied extensively~\cite{peikert2016decade}.

This paper studies an intermediate point. The public matrix is a \emph{Hankel} matrix $H_{i,j} = a_{i+j}$ generated by a single pseudorandom sequence $a$, so that multiplying by it computes a contiguous slice of the coefficients of a polynomial product in $\Zq[X]$, with no reduction modulo any polynomial. Which slice is published matters. The outermost coefficients of a linear convolution involve only a few secret coordinates, and publishing them would expose the secret one coordinate at a time. We therefore publish only the \emph{fully supported} coefficients, each of which involves all $n$ secret coordinates. This is the \emph{middle product} of Ro\c{s}ca, Sakzad, Steinfeld and Stehl\'e~\cite{rsss2017middle}.

Their Middle-Product LWE problem (MP-LWE) uses the same operation, but places the long operand on the secret side and draws many short public operands. That arrangement is what makes reductions from Ring-LWE possible, and it is also a large part of why MP-LWE schemes are big: the Titanium KEM~\cite{titanium2017nist,ssz2019practical} has a 16~KB public key. We invert the arrangement, publishing one long sequence and keeping the secret short, which brings a complete exchange down to 2{,}528 bytes. In exchange, we have no reduction. The security of the scheme rests on a new assumption, the hardness of the \emph{Windowed Hankel LWE} problem, which we support with a precise comparison to MP-LWE, an analysis of how known structure-exploiting attacks fare against it, and concrete estimates for every generic attack family.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A KEM built on windowed Hankel products, in which a 128-coordinate window carries a 256-bit message by 4-PAM modulation and the modulus $q = 2^{12}$ reduces all arithmetic to masking (Section~\ref{sec:scheme}).
  \item A definition of the underlying problem and an analysis of its structure: how it differs from MP-LWE, why published structure-exploiting attacks do not apply, an embedding into a cyclic ring, and the effect of the power-of-two modulus (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).
  \item A parameter study, concrete estimates for all generic attack families, and an exact Chernoff bound on the decryption failure rate (Sections~\ref{sec:params} and~\ref{sec:security}), with candidate parameters for NIST Categories~3 and~5 (Appendix~\ref{app:higher}).
  \item A constant-time C implementation that is bit-exact with the reference implementation, with a static audit of its compiled code and statistical timing tests (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).
\end{enumerate}

After reviewing related work (Section~\ref{sec:related}) and fixing notation (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}), we define the problem (Section~\ref{sec:problem}), specify the scheme and its parameters (Sections~\ref{sec:scheme} and~\ref{sec:params}), analyze its security (Section~\ref{sec:security}) and describe the implementation (Section~\ref{sec:impl}). Section~\ref{sec:limitations} closes with limitations and open questions.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
%=====================================================================

\paragraph{Structured LWE.} Ring-, Module- and Polynomial-LWE gain efficiency by working in polynomial rings~\cite{peikert2016decade}. Peikert and Pepin cast all of these, together with Order-LWE and MP-LWE, as instances of a single generalized LWE problem defined by a bilinear form, and give simpler and tighter reductions among them~\cite{peikert2019structured}. In their framework, the matrix slices of the MP-LWE bilinear form are exactly the standard basis of the Hankel matrices~\cite[Sect.~5]{peikert2019structured}, which is the precise sense in which our scheme is Hankel-structured.

\paragraph{Middle-Product LWE.} Ro\c{s}ca et al.\ introduced MP-LWE and reduced Polynomial-LWE to it for every defining polynomial in a large family, so that MP-LWE is at least as hard as the hardest of these Polynomial-LWE instances~\cite{rsss2017middle}. Steinfeld, Sakzad and Zhao tightened the reduction and used it to set the parameters of Titanium~\cite{ssz2019practical}, a first-round candidate in the NIST process that did not advance to the second round~\cite{nistir8240}. Peikert and Pepin reduce Ring-LWE to MP-LWE through Order-LWE with less error growth~\cite{peikert2019structured}, and three recent works widen the coverage further: to at least 90\% of bounded-coefficient polynomials of a given degree, experimentally~\cite{nchiwo2026improved}; to all number fields defined by monic irreducible polynomials with polynomially bounded coefficients~\cite{pelletmary2026unified}; and with less noise growth for trace-based reductions~\cite{cheng2026tighter}. Bai et al.\ prove hardness for secrets that are small relative to the modulus, which their signature scheme MPSign requires~\cite{bai2020mpsign}. Yang and Huang generalize the middle product to a universal product with more flexible parameters~\cite{yang2022universal}. MP-LWE also supports identity-based encryption~\cite{lvv2019trapdoors}; Tezuka and Tanaka survey these applications~\cite{tezuka2025survey}.

\paragraph{Cryptanalysis.} Three published attacks exploit structure of the kind considered here: a dimension reduction for MP-LWE by Steinfeld et al.~\cite{ssz2019practical}, a coefficient-by-coefficient recovery of small secrets by Bai et al.~\cite{bai2020mpsign}, and evaluation attacks on Ring-LWE over rings whose defining polynomial has a root of small order modulo $q$, by Elias, Lauter, Ozman and Stange~\cite{elos2015weak}. Section~\ref{sec:structure} explains why none of them applies directly. Compact-LWE, a lightweight LWE variant broken shortly after publication~\cite{bootle2018compact}, is a reminder that compact variants deserve suspicion. For concrete estimates we follow the Core-SVP methodology~\cite{adps2016newhope} and the analysis of Albrecht, Player and Scott~\cite{aps2015concrete}, as implemented in the lattice estimator~\cite{latticeestimator}, and also report the cost model of MATZOV~\cite{matzov2022}.

We found no published analysis of the setting studied here, with one long public sequence, a short binomial secret and narrow noise.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}
%=====================================================================

Throughout, $q = 4096$ and $\Zq = \Z/q\Z$, with elements represented in $[0, q)$, and vectors are indexed from $0$. $\SHAKE(x, \ell)$ denotes $\ell$ bytes of SHAKE256 output on input $x$~\cite{fips202}, $\SHA$ denotes SHA-256~\cite{fips180}, and $\|$ denotes concatenation. The centered binomial distribution $\CBD_\eta$ outputs $\sum_{i<\eta} (x_i - y_i)$ for independent uniform bits $x_i, y_i$; in particular, $\CBD_2$ takes the values $-2, \dots, 2$ with probabilities $(1, 4, 6, 4, 1)/16$ and has variance $1$. The scheme samples one $\CBD_2$ value from the low four bits of each byte of SHAKE256 output.

\subsection{Hankel matrices and the middle product}
\begin{definition}[Hankel matrix]
For $a = (a_0, \dots, a_{m+n-2}) \in \Zq^{m+n-1}$, the $m \times n$ \emph{Hankel matrix} of $a$ is $H_m(a) \in \Zq^{m \times n}$ with $H_m(a)_{i,j} = a_{i+j}$.
\end{definition}

Write $a(X) = \sum_t a_t X^t$, and let $\tilde{s}(X) = \sum_{j<n} s_j X^{n-1-j}$ be the reversal of $s \in \Zq^n$. Then
\[
(H_m(a)\, s)_i = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} a_{i+j}\, s_j = [X^{i+n-1}]\; a(X)\,\tilde{s}(X).
\]
Of the $m + 2n - 2$ coefficients of $a(X)\tilde{s}(X)$, the vector $H_m(a)\,s$ keeps the $m$ in the middle, which are exactly those involving all $n$ entries of $s$; this is the $m$-\emph{middle product} of~\cite{rsss2017middle}. Unlike circulant or negacyclic matrices, Hankel matrices are not closed under multiplication and do not form a ring.

Because convolution is commutative, we may also write $H_m(a)\,s = T_m(s)\,a$, where $T_m(s) \in \Zq^{m \times (m+n-1)}$ is the banded matrix with $T_m(s)_{i,t} = s_{t-i}$ for $0 \le t - i < n$ and $0$ otherwise. The two forms correspond to the two ways of assigning the long and the short operand, which is the distinction that matters in Section~\ref{sec:mplwe}.

\subsection{4-PAM modulation}
A 256-bit message is carried by $W = 128$ coordinates, two bits each. Symbol $\mu \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ is encoded as $\PAM(\mu) = \mu \cdot q/4 = 1024\mu$, and symbol $w$ of the message consists of bits $2(w \bmod 4)$ and $2(w \bmod 4) + 1$ of byte $\lfloor w/4 \rfloor$.

\begin{proposition}[Decoder]\label{prop:decoder}
If $v = 1024\mu + e \bmod 4096$ with $-512 \le e \le 511$, then $((v + 512) \bmod 4096) \gg 10 = \mu$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $v + 512 \equiv 1024\mu + (e + 512) \pmod{4096}$ with $0 \le e + 512 \le 1023$, we have $\lfloor ((v+512) \bmod 4096)/1024 \rfloor = \mu$, and division by $1024$ is a right shift by 10.
\end{proof}

\subsection{KEMs and the Fujisaki--Okamoto transform}
A KEM is secure if the shared key is indistinguishable from random even to an adversary with a decapsulation oracle (IND-CCA). The Fujisaki--Okamoto transform with implicit rejection builds such a KEM from a public-key encryption scheme that is IND-CPA secure and $\delta$-correct, where $\delta$ is the probability that decryption of an honestly generated ciphertext fails. Hofheinz, H\"ovelmanns and Kiltz analyze it in the random oracle model~\cite{hhk2017modular}, and Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang2018qrom} and Bindel et al.~\cite{bindel2019tighter} in the quantum random oracle model; in all of these bounds the adversary's advantage grows with $\delta$.

\section{The Windowed Hankel LWE Problem}\label{sec:problem}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{Definition}
\begin{definition}[Windowed Hankel LWE]\label{def:whlwe}
Let $n, k \ge 1$, $m_1, \dots, m_k \ge 1$ and $q \ge 2$, and let $\chi_s$ and $\chi_e$ be distributions on $\Z$. In $\WHLWE_{n,(m_1,\dots,m_k),q,\chi_s,\chi_e}$, a secret $s \gets \chi_s^n$ is sampled, and for each $\ell \le k$ independently a uniform $a^{(\ell)} \in \Zq^{n + m_\ell - 1}$ and an error $e^{(\ell)} \gets \chi_e^{m_\ell}$, giving $b^{(\ell)} = H_{m_\ell}(a^{(\ell)})\, s + e^{(\ell)} \bmod q$. The \emph{search} problem is to find $s$ from $(a^{(\ell)}, b^{(\ell)})_{\ell \le k}$; the \emph{decision} problem is to distinguish these pairs from pairs in which each $b^{(\ell)}$ is replaced by a uniform vector.
\end{definition}

The scheme relies on two instances, both with $n = 640$, $q = 4096$ and $\chi_s = \chi_e = \CBD_2$: the public key is an instance with $k = 1$ and $m_1 = 768$, and the ciphertext, once the public key has been replaced by a uniform vector in the security argument, is an instance with $k = 2$ and $(m_1, m_2) = (768, 128)$ whose secret is the ephemeral $r$ (Section~\ref{sec:kem-security}). Read as plain LWE, each is an instance with secret dimension $n$ and $m_1 + \dots + m_k$ samples whose matrix happens to be Hankel.

\subsection{Comparison with Middle-Product LWE}\label{sec:mplwe}
In MP-LWE~\cite[Def.~3.5]{rsss2017middle}, the secret is a uniform polynomial of degree less than $n + d - 1$, and the adversary receives arbitrarily many samples, each pairing a fresh uniform polynomial $a_i$ of degree less than $n$ with $d$ noisy coefficients of the middle product of $a_i$ and the secret. Ro\c{s}ca et al.\ observe that this is LWE with correlated samples, sample $i$ multiplying the secret by a banded $d \times (n + d - 1)$ Toeplitz matrix built from $a_i$~\cite[Sect.~3.2]{rsss2017middle}. In the language of Peikert and Pepin, sample $i$ applies a $d \times n$ Hankel matrix generated by the \emph{secret} to the coefficient vector of $a_i$~\cite[Def.~5.1]{peikert2019structured}. Our problem is the mirror image: the Hankel matrix is generated by the \emph{public} sequence and applied to the secret. Table~\ref{tab:mplwe} sets the two side by side.

The known reductions depend on the MP-LWE arrangement at every step. They embed a Polynomial-LWE secret into the long MP-LWE secret, rerandomize across many samples, and need noise wide enough to absorb the distortion of the embedding~\cite{rsss2017middle,peikert2019structured,nchiwo2026improved,pelletmary2026unified}; Ro\c{s}ca et al.\ also choose the width $\Omega(\sqrt{n})$ explicitly to rule out attacks in the style of Arora and Ge~\cite[Sect.~4]{rsss2017middle}. With a single public sequence, a short secret and noise of standard deviation $1$, none of these steps is available to us. Commutativity does not repair this: $H_m(a)\,s = T_m(s)\,a$ is an identity between matrices, not between security games, since the noise, the entropy and the choice of hidden operand all sit on different sides.

What can be said is limited. Windowed Hankel LWE is plain LWE whose matrix happens to be Hankel, so any algorithm for LWE with secret dimension $640$ and $768$ samples solves it; it is \emph{at most} as hard as plain LWE, which is why generic estimates are the optimistic direction. The converse fails for a dimension count: a Hankel matrix has $n + m - 1 = 1{,}407$ free entries against $nm = 491{,}520$ for a uniform matrix, so a generic LWE instance cannot be embedded in one. Nor is the public key statistically hidden, since the secret carries about $1{,}300$ bits of entropy while $b$ has $768 \times 12 = 9{,}216$ bits; hiding is computational and depends on the structure. Finally, the cyclic restatement of Section~\ref{sec:structure} does not import hardness from cyclotomic Ring-LWE: the ring $\Zq[X]/(X^L - 1)$ has the small-order root $1$, and relating it to a cyclotomic ring would mean working with complete samples, which is exactly what the withheld coefficients prevent. Windowed Hankel LWE thus lies off both branches of the structured-LWE landscape, neither unstructured nor inherited from a ring, and we treat its hardness as an assumption in its own right, checking resistance to Arora--Ge attacks concretely in Section~\ref{sec:security}.

\subsection{Structure and known attacks}\label{sec:structure}
A Hankel matrix $H_m(a)$ is determined by $n + m - 1$ values rather than $nm$, and its rows are shifts of one another. We now ask whether known attacks can exploit this; Section~\ref{sec:security} treats the generic attacks.

\paragraph{Full support defeats truncation and peeling.} Since $b_i = \sum_{j<n} a_{i+j} s_j + e_i$, every published coordinate depends on all $n$ secret coordinates: the structure of $H_m(a)$ forces none of its entries to be zero. This rules out two published techniques. Steinfeld et al.\ reduce the dimension of the primal attack on MP-LWE by discarding output coordinates, which removes secret coordinates because each output sees only a window of the long secret~\cite[Prop.~2]{ssz2019practical}. Here, discarding outputs removes no secret coordinate; it merely selects a subset of samples, which the generic attacks already optimize. Bai et al.\ recover a small secret coefficient by coefficient from the lowest coefficients of a full, noiseless product, where the $r$-th coefficient involves only secret coefficients of index at most $r$~\cite[Sect.~4]{bai2020mpsign}. Our window excludes exactly those coefficients, and our outputs are noisy.

\paragraph{Embedding into a cyclic ring.} Let $L = n + m - 1$ be the length of the public sequence. Reducing $a(X)\tilde{s}(X)$, of degree at most $L + n - 2$, modulo $X^L - 1$ folds only the coefficients of degree at least $L$, onto degrees $0, \dots, n - 2$. The published coefficients, of degrees $n - 1$ to $L - 1$, are untouched, so
\[
b_i = [X^{i+n-1}] \left( a(X)\,\tilde{s}(X) \bmod (X^L - 1) \right) + e_i, \qquad 0 \le i < m.
\]
A public key is therefore a Ring-LWE-type sample over $\Zq[X]/(X^L - 1)$ whose secret is supported on $n$ of the $L$ coefficients and of which only $m$ output coefficients are revealed. This ring is far from the cyclotomic rings recommended for Ring-LWE, since $X^L - 1$ has the root $1$ modulo every $q$. Over such rings, evaluating a sample at a root $\alpha$ of the defining polynomial collapses it to a one-dimensional sample $b(\alpha) = a(\alpha)s(\alpha) + e(\alpha)$, and this is the basis of the attacks of Elias et al.~\cite{elos2015weak}.

The evaluation, however, needs all $L$ coefficients of the sample, and $n - 1 = 639$ of them are withheld. With only the window, an attacker can apply a linear functional $\varphi \in \Z^m$ and obtain
\[
\varphi^{\top} b = \bigl(H_m(a)^{\top} \varphi\bigr)^{\top} s + \varphi^{\top} e .
\]
For a factor $g$ of $X^L - 1$ over $\Zq$ of degree $k$, let $R_g \in \Zq^{k \times n}$ be the matrix of the map $s \mapsto \tilde{s} \bmod g$. Functionals with $H_m(a)^{\top}\varphi = R_g^{\top} c$ for some $c \in \Zq^k$ give $\varphi^{\top} b = c^{\top}(\tilde{s} \bmod g) + \varphi^{\top} e$, which depends on $s$ only through the $k$ coefficients of $\tilde{s} \bmod g$; for $g = X - 1$ this is $s(1) = \sum_j s_j$. The all-ones functional does not qualify, because the entries $\sum_{i<m} a_{i+j}$ of $H_m(a)^{\top}\mathbf{1}$ vary with $j$: truncation does not commute with evaluation. The qualifying functionals form a $q$-ary lattice $\Lambda_g$ of rank $m$, cut out by $n - k$ linear constraints modulo $q$. To use them, an attacker needs vectors of $\Lambda_g$ short enough that $\varphi^{\top} e$ is far from uniform, and must then guess $\tilde{s} \bmod g$.

This is the situation of the dual hybrid attack of Section~\ref{sec:security}, which likewise drops $n - k$ constraints and guesses $k$ quantities, except that there the guessed quantities are $k$ coordinates of $s$. Since $1407 = 3 \cdot 7 \cdot 67$ and $2$ has order $2$, $3$, $6$ and $66$ modulo $3$, $7$, $21$ and $67$, the polynomial $X^{1407} - 1$ factors over $\Zq$ into irreducible factors of degrees $1$, $2$, $3$, $3$, $6$ and $6$ and twenty-one factors of degree $66$. Appendix~\ref{app:functionals} reports two computations for the low-degree factors. At full size, $\Lambda_g$ has determinant exactly $q^{n-k}$ for every random sequence tested, the same as the corresponding dual-hybrid lattice, while the quantity to be guessed carries more entropy than $k$ coordinates of $s$: $6.7$ bits for $s(1)$ against $2.0$ bits for one coordinate, and $36.0$ against $6.1$ bits for each cubic factor. At reduced dimensions, where shortest vectors can be computed exactly, the lattices $\Lambda_g$ built from Hankel matrices have shortest vectors within a few percent of the Gaussian heuristic, like lattices built from uniform matrices: over 315 instances of each kind, the mean ratio lies between $0.99$ and $1.02$ for every factor and size. Functionals of this kind therefore give an attacker nothing that the generic dual hybrid attack does not already provide more cheaply. This does not exclude other uses of the ring structure, which remain open.

\paragraph{Power-of-two modulus.} Reducing a sample modulo $2^k$, for $k < 12$, gives an LWE instance with the same secret and error modulo $2^k$. For small $k$ such an instance does not even determine $s$. Modulo $2$, the error is uniform, since odd values of $\CBD_2$ have total probability $1/2$, so $b \bmod 2$ is independent of $s$. Modulo $4$ and $8$, the error has entropy $1.906$ and $2.031$ bits, so each of the $768$ samples carries at most $0.094$ and $0.969$ bits of information about $s \bmod 2^k$: at most $72$ and $744$ bits in total, against an entropy of $1{,}220$ and $1{,}300$ bits for $s \bmod 4$ and $s \bmod 8$. For larger $k$, the reduced instance has a smaller ratio of modulus to noise than the original, which makes lattice attacks harder, not easier.

\paragraph{Reuse of the public sequence.} The sequence $a$ generates both the public key and the first component of every ciphertext, much as ML-KEM reuses its matrix. Each key pair has its own seed, so no structure is shared between users.

\section{The Scheme}\label{sec:scheme}
%=====================================================================

The parameters are $n = 640$, $W = 128$, $m = n + W = 768$, $q = 4096$ and $\eta = 2$; the public sequence has length $L = 2n + W - 1 = 1407$, and $H = H_m(a)$.

\subsection{Public-key encryption}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{$\mathsf{CPA.KeyGen}(\sigma)$}\label{alg:cpa_keygen}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\sigma \in \{0,1\}^{256}$
\State $(\mathit{seed}_a \,\|\, \mathit{seed}_e) \gets \SHAKE(\sigma \,\|\, \texttt{"\_hankel\_cpa\_keygen\_seed"}, 64)$
\State $a \gets (\SHAKE(\mathit{seed}_a, 2L)$ read as little-endian 16-bit words$) \bmod q$
\State $\beta \gets \SHAKE(\mathit{seed}_e \,\|\, \texttt{"\_hankel\_noise"}, n + m)$
\State $s \gets \CBD_2(\beta[0{:}n])$; \ $e \gets \CBD_2(\beta[n{:}n+m])$
\State $b \gets H s + e \bmod q$
\State \Return $\mathit{pk} = (\mathit{seed}_a, b)$, \ $\mathit{sk} = s$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{$\mathsf{CPA.Enc}(\mathit{pk}, M; c)$}\label{alg:cpa_enc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\mathit{pk} = (\mathit{seed}_a, b)$, message $M \in \{0,1\}^{256}$, coins $c \in \{0,1\}^{256}$
\State Regenerate $a$ from $\mathit{seed}_a$
\State $\gamma \gets \SHAKE(c \,\|\, \texttt{"\_hankel\_encaps\_coins"}, n + m + W)$
\State $r \gets \CBD_2(\gamma[0{:}n])$; \ $e_1 \gets \CBD_2(\gamma[n{:}n+m])$; \ $e_2 \gets \CBD_2(\gamma[n+m{:}n+m+W])$
\State $u \gets H r + e_1 \bmod q$
\State $K_w \gets \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} r_j\, b_{w+j} \bmod q$ \ for $0 \le w < W$ \Comment{$K = H_W(b)\, r$}
\State $v \gets K + \PAM(M) + e_2 \bmod q$
\State \Return $(u, v)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For compatibility with the reference implementation, key generation also sets $s_0 = 1$ if $s = 0$, without branching. The event has probability $2^{-905.6}$ and has no effect on the analysis.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{$\mathsf{CPA.Dec}(s, (u, v))$}\label{alg:cpa_dec}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $K'_w \gets \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} s_k\, u_{w+k} \bmod q$ \ for $0 \le w < W$ \Comment{$K' = H_W(u)\, s$}
\State \Return $\mathrm{Decode}(v - K' \bmod q)$ \Comment{Proposition~\ref{prop:decoder}, coordinate-wise}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Correctness.} Substituting $b = Hs + e$ and $u = Hr + e_1$ gives
\[
K_w = \sum_{j,k} a_{w+j+k}\, r_j s_k + \sum_j r_j e_{w+j},
\qquad
K'_w = \sum_{j,k} a_{w+j+k}\, r_j s_k + \sum_k s_k e_{1,w+k}.
\]
The double sums agree because each term depends only on $j + k$; this is the Hankel counterpart of the identity $r^{\top}(As) = (r^{\top}A)s$ behind LWE-based key exchange~\cite{lindner2011better}. Hence
\[
v_w - K'_w = \PAM(M)_w + \underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} r_j e_{w+j} - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} s_k e_{1,w+k} + e_{2,w}}_{e_{\mathrm{dec},w}} \pmod q,
\]
and by Proposition~\ref{prop:decoder} decryption succeeds whenever $-512 \le e_{\mathrm{dec},w} \le 511$ for every $w$. The term $e_2$ cannot be omitted: without it, $v - \PAM(M) = H_W(b)\,r$ would hand an attacker $W$ noiseless linear equations in the ephemeral secret $r$.

\subsection{The KEM}
The KEM applies the Fujisaki--Okamoto transform with implicit rejection~\cite{hhk2017modular}. The secret key holds $s$ as signed bytes, a rejection value $z$, and a copy of the public key.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{$\mathsf{KeyGen}()$}\label{alg:keygen}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\sigma \gets \{0,1\}^{512}$
\State $(\mathit{pk}, s) \gets \mathsf{CPA.KeyGen}(\sigma[0{:}256])$
\State $z \gets \SHAKE(\sigma[256{:}512] \,\|\, \texttt{"\_hankel\_cca\_reject"}, 32)$
\State \Return $\mathit{pk}$, \ $\mathit{sk} = (s, z, \mathit{pk})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{$\mathsf{Encaps}(\mathit{pk})$}\label{alg:encaps}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $M \gets \{0,1\}^{256}$
\State $(\bar{K} \,\|\, c) \gets \SHAKE(M \,\|\, \SHA(\mathit{pk}), 64)$
\State $\mathit{ct} \gets \mathsf{CPA.Enc}(\mathit{pk}, M; c)$
\State \Return $\mathit{ct}$, \ $K = \SHAKE(\bar{K} \,\|\, \SHA(\mathit{ct}), 32)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{$\mathsf{Decaps}(\mathit{sk}, \mathit{ct})$}\label{alg:decaps}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Parse $\mathit{sk} = (s, z, \mathit{pk})$
\State $M' \gets \mathsf{CPA.Dec}(s, \mathit{ct})$
\State $(\bar{K}' \,\|\, c') \gets \SHAKE(M' \,\|\, \SHA(\mathit{pk}), 64)$
\State $\mathit{ct}' \gets \mathsf{CPA.Enc}(\mathit{pk}, M'; c')$
\State $\hat{K} \gets \bar{K}'$ if $\mathit{ct} = \mathit{ct}'$, else $z$ \Comment{constant-time comparison and selection}
\State \Return $K = \SHAKE(\hat{K} \,\|\, \SHA(\mathit{ct}), 32)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

A ciphertext with the wrong length or a malformed header can never equal a re-encryption, so it is rejected implicitly like any other invalid ciphertext. Appendix~\ref{app:wire} gives the byte-level formats.

\subsection{Security of the scheme}\label{sec:kem-security}
The encryption scheme is IND-CPA secure if the decision problems of Section~\ref{sec:problem} are hard, by the usual argument for LWE-based encryption~\cite{lindner2011better}. Decision $\WHLWE_{640,(768),q,\CBD_2,\CBD_2}$ lets us replace the public key $b$ by a uniform vector. The ciphertext then consists of $u = H_m(a)\,r + e_1$ and $v - \PAM(M) = H_W(b)\,r + e_2$, with $a$ and $b$ uniform and independent; since $H_W(b)$ uses only the first $n + W - 1 = 767$ of the 768 entries of $b$, this is an instance of decision $\WHLWE_{640,(768,128),q,\CBD_2,\CBD_2}$ with secret $r$, and replacing it by uniform hides $M$. The order of the steps matters. In a real ciphertext, the matrix $H_W(b)$ of $v$ is generated by $b = Hs + e$ and is therefore related to the matrix $H$ of $u$; an attack that exploited this relation to eliminate $r$ would distinguish $b$ from uniform, and so would break the public-key instance.

IND-CCA security then follows from IND-CPA security and the correctness error, in the random oracle model~\cite{hhk2017modular} and in the quantum random oracle model~\cite{jiang2018qrom,bindel2019tighter}; Section~\ref{sec:dfr} shows $\delta \le 2^{-134.1}$. Hashing $\SHA(\mathit{pk})$ into the coins binds each encapsulation to its public key, and hashing $\SHA(\mathit{ct})$ into the shared key binds the key to its ciphertext.

\section{Security Analysis}\label{sec:security}

\subsection{Methodology}
We treat each instance of Section~\ref{sec:problem} as unstructured LWE with secret dimension $n = 640$, modulus $q = 4096$, and secret and error of standard deviation $1$, which amounts to assuming that the structure gives the attacker no advantage (Section~\ref{sec:structure} examines that assumption). Costs come from the lattice estimator~\cite{latticeestimator} (commit \texttt{53da598}, August 2026) under two models for BKZ with block size $\beta$: the Core-SVP model~\cite{adps2016newhope}, which charges $2^{0.292\beta}$ classically per call to an SVP oracle, and the more detailed model of MATZOV~\cite{matzov2022}. The same version of the estimator reproduces exactly the Kyber-512 figures in its documentation, and we computed every figure in this section in two SageMath installations (10.8 and 10.9) with identical results.

The dual hybrid attack is the cheapest in both models. In the Core-SVP model it runs BKZ with $\beta = 468$, so lattice reduction alone costs $2^{0.292 \cdot 468} = 2^{136.7}$ classically and $2^{0.265 \cdot 468} = 2^{124.0}$ quantumly. The primal attack needs $\beta = 488$ in a lattice of dimension $1{,}194$, at $2^{142.5}$ classically and $2^{129.3}$ quantumly; for Kyber-512, whose Category~1 claim rests on a classical Core-SVP cost of $2^{118}$~\cite{bos2018kyber}, the estimator gives a matching primal figure. Across the table, our instance costs at least 20 bits more than Kyber-512 under the same model, the smallest margin being 20.6 bits for the dual hybrid attack under MATZOV. This margin is the whole budget for an attack that exploits the structure. The most natural such attack, through functionals derived from the cyclic embedding, reduces to the dual hybrid attack with a more expensive guess (Section~\ref{sec:structure} and Appendix~\ref{app:functionals}).

None of the remaining attack families comes close to the dual hybrid attack. The bounded support of the error lets each sample be written as a polynomial equation of degree $5$ in $s$, which is the starting point of Arora--Ge attacks. Linearizing these equations would take about $\binom{n+5}{5} \approx 2^{39.7}$ samples, far more than the 768 available, and with 768 samples the estimator's Gr\"obner-basis model reaches a degree of regularity of $442$, at a cost of about $2^{2100}$. The primal hybrid attack is no cheaper than the plain primal attacks ($2^{143.2}$ and $2^{163.1}$ in the two models), and its meet-in-the-middle variant costs $2^{283.4}$. The meet-in-the-middle dual hybrid, designed for sparse or ternary secrets, costs $2^{748.0}$ against $\CBD_2$.

Finally, none of the optimal attacks uses all the samples on offer: the primal attack uses 553, the dual attack about 620, and the dual hybrid attack 640, out of 768. The 896 samples that a ciphertext provides in the secret $r$ therefore do not lower the estimates either.

\subsection{Decryption failure rate}\label{sec:dfr}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:dfr}
The decryption failure rate satisfies $\delta \le 2^{-134.1}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For each window coordinate $w$, $e_{\mathrm{dec},w}$ is a sum of 1{,}281 independent terms: 1{,}280 products $XY$ of independent $\CBD_2$ variables, namely $r_j e_{w+j}$ and $-s_k e_{1,w+k}$ (the distribution of $XY$ is symmetric), and the $\CBD_2$ variable $e_{2,w}$. Each term has mean $0$ and variance $1$, so $\sigma = \sqrt{1281} \approx 35.79$ and the threshold $512$ lies $14.31\sigma$ from the mean. The Chernoff bound with the exact moment-generating functions (Appendix~\ref{app:chernoff}) gives $\Pr[e_{\mathrm{dec},w} \ge 512] \le 2^{-142.1}$, so by symmetry $e_{\mathrm{dec},w}$ leaves $[-512, 511]$ with probability at most $2^{-141.1}$. A union bound over the $W = 2^7$ coordinates gives $\delta \le 2^{-134.1}$.
\end{proof}

As usual, the bound is an average over key pairs. FIPS~203 lists $2^{-138.8}$ for ML-KEM-512~\cite[Table~1]{fips203}. Experiments cannot confirm a rate this small, but they agree with the bound: over 500 end-to-end exchanges (64{,}000 decoded coordinates), the largest $|e_{\mathrm{dec}}|$ was 131, and its mean and standard deviation, 28.4 and 21.4, match the values 28.5 and 21.6 predicted by a normal approximation.

Failure rate matters beyond correctness, because attacks that collect decryption failures can erode the security of LWE-based schemes whose failure rate is relatively high~\cite{danvers2019failures}. The 128 window coordinates share $r$, $s$ and $e_1$, so their errors are correlated. The union bound in Theorem~\ref{thm:dfr} remains valid, and is if anything conservative, but a failure, should one occur, reveals more about the secret than an independent-coordinate model would suggest. At $\delta \le 2^{-134.1}$ this is moot in practice: an attacker who submits $2^{64}$ honestly generated ciphertexts expects fewer than $2^{-70}$ failures.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}
%=====================================================================

We implemented the KEM twice: a Python reference and a C implementation of the complete scheme. The C code is bit-exact with the reference. It reproduces all fifteen known-answer test vectors, which come from a custom SHAKE-based generator rather than the NIST DRBG, and matches the reference on random inputs, on every branch of its seed handling and on malformed ciphertexts.

\subsection{Arithmetic}
All three Hankel products have the form $y_i = \sum_{j<n} a_{i+j} x_j \;(+\, e_i) \bmod q$. Because $q = 2^{12}$ divides $2^{16}$, they can be computed modulo $2^{16}$, using the natural wraparound of 16-bit integers, and reduced modulo $q$ by a single mask at the end; this is exact for any 16-bit inputs, including negative secret coefficients in two's complement. On ARMv8, the kernel computes 64 outputs at a time in eight 8-lane accumulators. For each secret coefficient $x_j$ it adds $x_j \cdot (a_{i+j}, \dots, a_{i+j+63})$ to the accumulators with \texttt{vmlaq\_laneq\_u16}, so a 768-output product takes $768 \cdot 640 / 8 = 61{,}440$ multiply-accumulate instructions, and the secret only ever enters as a multiplication operand. A portable scalar kernel is kept and tested alongside.

\subsection{Hashing}
SHAKE256 uses a Keccak-$f[1600]$ permutation with fixed loop counts and no table lookups. SHA-256 only ever hashes public data, the public key and the ciphertext; it uses the ARMv8 SHA-256 instructions when available, and is checked against a portable implementation.

\subsection{Constant-time execution}
No secret value selects a branch or a memory address. The all-zero-secret fix-up of Section~\ref{sec:scheme}, the comparison $\mathit{ct} = \mathit{ct}'$ and the selection of $\hat{K}$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:decaps} use masks behind a compiler value barrier, so that the compiler cannot turn them back into branches. Every step that handles secrets is compiled as a separate function, which makes two kinds of check possible.

The first is a static audit of the compiled ARM64 code: a flow-sensitive dataflow analysis tracks which registers, stack slots and condition flags may depend on secret data, and reports any conditional branch on such a value, any division and any unexpected call. Across the nine secret-handling functions (1{,}274 instructions), it finds 23 conditional branches, all on public values, and no divisions or calls. It does not check memory addressing, but every index in these functions is a loop counter, which we verified by inspection.

The second is a set of fixed-versus-random Welch $t$-tests with about 4{,}000 calls per class, varying only the secret input: the seed for key generation, the message entropy for encapsulation, and, for decapsulation of a fixed ciphertext, the secret key, so that one class accepts and the other rejects. All three give $|t| < 4.5$. Repeating the decapsulation test with 20{,}000 calls per class, including a control that compares a class with itself, gave $|t| \le 2.3$ and medians within 10~ns: decapsulation takes the same time whether it accepts or rejects. This evidence covers timing only, on a single core with a single compiler; we have not studied power or electromagnetic leakage.

\subsection{Performance}
On an Apple M3 Max, with Apple clang 17 at \texttt{-O3}, the median latencies over 5{,}000 calls with fresh inputs are 14~$\mu$s for key generation, 17~$\mu$s for encapsulation and 20~$\mu$s for decapsulation, or 51~$\mu$s for a full handshake. An encapsulation spends about 8~$\mu$s in the two Hankel products and about 6~$\mu$s in SHAKE256, mostly expanding the public sequence. Faster Keccak would help, and so would subquadratic multiplication; without an NTT, the products stay quadratic in $n$. We do not compare timings with other schemes, which we did not measure under the same conditions.

The implementation is available at \url{https://github.com/Cryptozoa/windowed-hankel-kem}, together with the scripts that produce every figure in this paper and their raw outputs.

\section{Limitations and Open Questions}\label{sec:limitations}
%=====================================================================

The central limitation is the one we accepted by design: Windowed Hankel LWE has no reduction, and its hardness is an assumption. The estimates of Section~\ref{sec:security} hold only if the structure gives an attacker no advantage, and the roughly 20-bit margin over Kyber-512 is all that a structural attack would need to overcome. We have checked the published structure-exploiting attacks and the dual-type functionals that arise from the embedding into $\Zq[X]/(X^L - 1)$, and found no advantage, but this does not rule out other attacks. Several directions remain open: dual attacks that look for functionals merely \emph{biased} toward a low-dimensional function of the secret, rather than exactly isolating one; primal (bounded-distance decoding) attacks that use the subrings $\Zq[X]/(g)$ while treating the 639 withheld coefficients as unknowns; algebraic decoding, since a noise-free Hankel system is a Pad\'e-type problem that the extended Euclidean algorithm solves in $O(n^2)$ operations and a version tolerating small noise would be a new attack; and attacks on the joint distribution of a public key and its ciphertexts. We also have no search-to-decision reduction, and the usual arguments favor a prime modulus. The setting has received little study, and the fate of Compact-LWE~\cite{bootle2018compact} counsels caution; we offer the scheme as a problem for cryptanalysis, not for use, until it has been analyzed independently.

Two further limitations are practical. Without an NTT, multiplication costs $O(nm)$ operations, about $4.9 \times 10^5$ multiply-accumulates at $n = 640$. That is fast enough here, but it scales less well than ring-based schemes at higher security levels. Our constant-time evidence covers only timing, on one platform (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=====================================================================
Publishing a single long sequence and keeping the secret short brings an LWE-based KEM to 2.5~KB per exchange without reducing products modulo any polynomial, at the cost of the MP-LWE reductions. If the structure gives an attacker nothing, generic attacks cost at least 20 bits more than against Kyber-512. The published structural attacks do not transfer, and the most natural new one, through the hidden cyclic embedding, reduces to a generic attack with a more expensive guess. The structure has yet to face sustained independent cryptanalysis, and we hope the precise problem statement given here makes that analysis easier to undertake.

\section{The Chernoff Bound}\label{app:chernoff}
%=====================================================================
Let $Y = XX'$ for independent $X, X' \sim \CBD_2$. Its distribution is
\[
\Pr[Y = 0] = \tfrac{156}{256}, \quad \Pr[Y = \pm 1] = \tfrac{32}{256}, \quad \Pr[Y = \pm 2] = \tfrac{16}{256}, \quad \Pr[Y = \pm 4] = \tfrac{2}{256},
\]
with mean $0$ and variance $1$. Write $e_{\mathrm{dec},w} = \sum_{i=1}^{1280} Y_i + Z$ with $Z \sim \CBD_2$. Markov's inequality applied to $e^{t e_{\mathrm{dec},w}}$ gives, for every $t > 0$,
\[
\Pr[e_{\mathrm{dec},w} \ge 512] \le \exp\bigl(1280 \ln M_Y(t) + \ln M_Z(t) - 512\,t\bigr),
\]
where $M_Y(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]$ is computed exactly from the distribution above and $M_Z(t) = \cosh^4(t/2)$. The exponent is convex in $t$ and minimized at $t^{*} \approx 0.3715$, where the bound equals $2^{-142.07}$. The same computation, with $1280$ replaced by $2n$, the threshold by $q/2^{b+1}$ for $b$ bits per coordinate and the union bound taken over $256/b$ coordinates, produces every entry of Table~\ref{tab:dfr-grid}.

\section{Short Functionals from the Cyclic Embedding}\label{app:functionals}
%=====================================================================
This appendix gives the computations behind the embedding argument of Section~\ref{sec:structure}.

\paragraph{The lattice.} Let $g$ be a monic factor of $X^L - 1$ over $\Zq$ of degree $k$, and let $R_g \in \Zq^{k \times n}$ have as its column $j$ the coefficient vector of $X^{n-1-j} \bmod g$, so that $R_g s$ is the coefficient vector of $\tilde{s} \bmod g$. The last $k$ columns of $R_g$ are unit vectors, so the condition $H_m(a)^{\top}\varphi = R_g^{\top} c$ determines $c$ from $k$ coordinates of $H_m(a)^{\top}\varphi$ and reduces to $n - k$ linear conditions $B_g \varphi \equiv 0 \pmod q$, with $B_g \in \Zq^{(n-k) \times m}$. The functionals form the lattice $\Lambda_g = \{\varphi \in \Z^m : B_g \varphi \equiv 0 \pmod q\}$; for $k = 0$ it is the lattice of the plain dual attack. If $B_g$ has full rank modulo $2$, it maps $\Z^m$ onto $\Zq^{n-k}$ and $\det \Lambda_g = q^{n-k}$, exactly as for a uniform matrix, and as for the dual-hybrid lattice obtained by guessing $k$ coordinates of $s$. We estimate the shortest vector by the Gaussian heuristic $\mathrm{GH}(\Lambda_g) = \Gamma(m/2 + 1)^{1/m} \det(\Lambda_g)^{1/m} / \sqrt{\pi}$. The entropy of $\tilde{s} \bmod g$ is estimated with a Gaussian of covariance $R_g R_g^{\top}$, using centered representatives, and capped at $12$ bits per coefficient; for the cubic and sextic factors the entries of $R_g$ are large modulo $q$, the cap applies, and $\tilde{s} \bmod g$ is close to uniform.

\paragraph{Full size.} Table~\ref{tab:func-full} covers $n = 640$, $m = 768$ and five random sequences $a$. In every case $B_g$ has full rank modulo $2$, so the structured lattices are exactly as dense as the corresponding dual-hybrid lattices, and the comparison reduces to the guess. The Gaussian heuristic also places the shortest functionals above $q$: $\varphi^{\top} e$ then has standard deviation larger than the modulus, so a single functional reveals almost nothing, and any use must combine many of them, as the dual attack does. We do not convert these figures into an attack cost; that is what the estimates of Section~\ref{sec:security} provide for the dual hybrid attack, which, by the argument above, these functionals do not beat.

\section{Higher Security Categories}\label{app:higher}
%=====================================================================
This appendix derives candidate parameters for NIST Categories~3 and~5 by the method of Section~\ref{sec:params}. We size them as the Category~1 set is sized: the cheapest estimated attack should exceed that on the corresponding Kyber parameter set by at least 20 bits in both cost models, and the failure rate should be no larger than ML-KEM's. Under the estimator and models of Section~\ref{sec:security}, the cheapest attacks on Kyber-768 and Kyber-1024 cost $2^{174.3}$ and $2^{241.8}$ (Core-SVP) and $2^{196.4}$ and $2^{262.3}$ (MATZOV); FIPS~203 lists failure rates of $2^{-164.8}$ and $2^{-174.8}$ for ML-KEM-768 and ML-KEM-1024~\cite[Table~1]{fips203}. The message stays at 256 bits.

\paragraph{The Category 1 recipe does not scale.} The decryption error has variance $2n + 1$, so the failure rate grows with $n$. With $q = 2^{12}$, $\CBD_2$ and 4-PAM, the Chernoff bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:dfr} exceeds $2^{-164.8}$ for every $n \ge 640$; at $n = 640$ it is already $2^{-134.1}$. Four modifications reach the targets:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[A.] $q = 2^{13}$, keeping $\CBD_2$ and 4-PAM, at the cost of one more bit per coefficient;
  \item[B.] one bit per coordinate ($W = 256$), keeping $q = 2^{12}$ and $\CBD_2$;
  \item[C.] $\CBD_1$ secret and noise, keeping $q = 2^{12}$ and 4-PAM;
  \item[D.] $q = 2^{13}$ with $\CBD_3$ and one bit per coordinate.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:families} gives the smallest $n$ that meets the targets in each family. Family~C is the smallest, but its secret is the sparse ternary one set aside in Section~\ref{sec:params}. Families~A and~B are within 102 bytes of each other at both levels. We choose family~A because it keeps the Category~1 scheme unchanged except for $q$, and because both of its lengths $L$ are prime with $X - 1$ as the only small factor of $X^L - 1$; for family~B at Category~5, $L = 2{,}511$ gives factors of degrees 2, 5, 6, 10 and 18.

\paragraph{Ring structure.} The dimension $n$ of Windowed Hankel LWE can be any integer, and the factorization of $X^L - 1$ varies widely with it. For example, $n = 960$ in family~A gives $L = 2{,}047 = 2^{11} - 1$, for which $X^L - 1$ has 186 irreducible factors of degree~11. The proposed sets happen to fall on the favorable side: $L = 1{,}999$ and $L = 2{,}543$ are prime, and $2$ has order $333$ and $1{,}271$ modulo them, so $X - 1$ is the only factor of $X^L - 1$ of degree below $333$. Of the low-degree factors examined in Appendix~\ref{app:functionals}, only $X - 1$ remains. Repeating that check at the new sizes gives the same result: for five random sequences each, the lattices of functionals for the plain dual attack and for $X - 1$ have full rank modulo~$2$, hence the determinants of the corresponding dual-hybrid lattices, while isolating $s(1)$ requires guessing $7.0$ and $7.2$ bits against $2.0$ bits for a coordinate of $s$. Choosing $L$ prime with $2$ of large order is a free design rule for any further parameter set.

\paragraph{Implementation.} Both sets are built from the same C code as the Category~1 set (Section~\ref{sec:impl}), compiled once per set. Since $2^{13}$ divides $2^{16}$, accumulating in 16-bit lanes stays exact, and the only changes are the packing, at 13 bits per coefficient and least significant bit first, and the 4-PAM scaling by $q/4$. To keep the sets apart, every derivation tag of Appendix~\ref{app:wire} is followed by a domain string, \texttt{\_wh936} or \texttt{\_wh1208}; the tags of the Category~1 set are unchanged. Each set has 15 known-answer vectors, generated by a parameterized Python reference, and the C code reproduces them exactly. It passes the same static branch audit and fixed-versus-random timing tests as the Category~1 code, with $|t| < 4.5$ in all six tests. On the machine of Section~\ref{sec:impl}, median latencies over 5{,}000 calls are 30, 31 and 37~$\mu$s for key generation, encapsulation and decapsulation at Category~3, and 40, 48 and 48~$\mu$s at Category~5, or about 100 and 135~$\mu$s for a full handshake.

\paragraph{What scaling does not change.} These sets rest on the same assumption as the Category~1 set, and a larger $n$ adds no evidence for it. The open questions of Section~\ref{sec:limitations} apply unchanged, and they matter more, not less, at the levels intended for long-term security.
